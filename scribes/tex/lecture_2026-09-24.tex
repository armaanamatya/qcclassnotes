In this lecture, we see the first quantum algorithms that beat every classical algorithm in the
query model. We study Deutsch's algorithm, which decides with a single query whether a function
$f \colon \set{0,1} \to \set{0,1}$ is constant or balanced, the \emph{phase flip} form of a quantum
query, the $n$-qubit Hadamard (Walsh--Hadamard) transform, and the Deutsch--Jozsa algorithm, which
does the same for $f \colon \set{0,1}^n \to \set{0,1}$, again with a single query.

\section{Deutsch's Algorithm}

\begin{problem}[Deutsch's Problem]
    Given a function $f \colon \set{0,1} \to \set{0,1}$ as a black box, determine whether $f$ is
    \emph{constant} ($f(0) = f(1)$) or \emph{balanced} ($f(0) \neq f(1)$).
\end{problem}

Classically we need $2$ queries: after one query, say $f(0)$, both answers are still possible.
Quantumly, Deutsch's algorithm does it with $1$ query.

\subsection{The Quantum Query \texorpdfstring{$U_f$}{Uf}}

A quantum circuit is reversible, but $f$ need not be (a constant $f$ forgets its input). We therefore
convert $f$ into a reversible \emph{quantum version} $U_f$: a controlled-$f$ gate modeled on the
CNOT gate of \cref{def:cnot-0915}. Where CNOT XORs the control bit $a$ into the target, $U_f$ XORs
$f(a)$ into it (\cref{fig:uf-0924}):
\[U_f \colon \ket{a}\ket{b} \longmapsto \ket{a}\ket{b \oplus f(a)}.\]
The first qubit (the input $a$) is left unchanged, and $f(a)$ is XORed into the second qubit.
Applying $U_f$ twice gives back $\ket{a}\ket{b}$, so $U_f$ is reversible. One application of $U_f$
counts as one query to $f$.

\subsection{The Circuit}

Deutsch's algorithm is usually given in the form of \cref{fig:deutsch-0924}: the first qubit starts in
$\ket{0}$ and the second in $\ket{1}$; both go through a Hadamard gate, then through $U_f$, then
through a Hadamard gate again, and are measured. We will show that the measurement of the first qubit
gives $\ket{0}$ if $f$ is constant and $\ket{1}$ if $f$ is balanced (the second qubit always gives
$\ket{1}$).

The action of the Hadamard gate on $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ (\cref{eq:hadamard-0915}) can be written
in one formula: for $a \in \set{0,1}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hadamard-0924}
    H\ket{a} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} + (-1)^{a}\ket{1}\right)
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_{b \in \set{0,1}} (-1)^{ab} \ket{b}.
\end{equation}
Since $H^2 = I$ (\cref{lem:hh-0915}), applying $H$ to the right-hand side of \cref{eq:hadamard-0924}
gives back $\ket{a}$.

\begin{theorem}[Deutsch's Algorithm]\label{thm:deutsch-0924}
    The circuit of \cref{fig:deutsch-0924} makes one query to $f$, and measuring its first qubit gives
    $f(0) \oplus f(1)$ with probability $1$. Hence the outcome is $0$ if $f$ is constant and $1$ if $f$
    is balanced.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    After the first two Hadamard gates the state is (by \cref{eq:mixed-0915})
    \begin{align*}
        \ket{\psi_1}
         & = H\ket{0} \otimes H\ket{1}
        = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} + \ket{1}\right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right) \\
         & = \frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right) + \ket{1}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)\right).
    \end{align*}
    Applying $U_f$ to each term, and using that
    $\ket{0 \oplus c} - \ket{1 \oplus c} = (-1)^{c}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)$ for $c \in \set{0,1}$
    (for $c = 1$ the two kets are swapped),
    \begin{align*}
        \ket{\psi_2} = U_f \ket{\psi_1}
         & = \frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0}\left(\ket{0 \oplus f(0)} - \ket{1 \oplus f(0)}\right)
        + \ket{1}\left(\ket{0 \oplus f(1)} - \ket{1 \oplus f(1)}\right)\right)                        \\
         & = \frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0}(-1)^{f(0)}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)
        + \ket{1}(-1)^{f(1)}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)\right)                                    \\
         & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left((-1)^{f(0)}\ket{0} + (-1)^{f(1)}\ket{1}\right)
        \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)                                     \\
         & = (-1)^{f(0)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} + (-1)^{f(0) \oplus f(1)}\ket{1}\right)
        \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right),
    \end{align*}
    where in the last step we used $(-1)^{f(1)} = (-1)^{f(0)} (-1)^{f(0) \oplus f(1)}$. The first qubit
    is now exactly the right-hand side of \cref{eq:hadamard-0924} with $a = f(0) \oplus f(1)$, up to the
    sign $(-1)^{f(0)}$. Applying $H$ to it therefore gives
    \[\ket{\psi_3} = (-1)^{f(0)} \ket{f(0) \oplus f(1)} \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right).\]
    The factor $(-1)^{f(0)}$ is a global phase and does not affect the measurement, so measuring the
    first qubit gives $f(0) \oplus f(1)$ with probability $1$. (Applying $H$ to the second qubit gives
    $\ket{1}$, which is why the second measurement in \cref{fig:deutsch-0924} always gives $1$.)
\end{proof}

\section{Phase Flip and the \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-Qubit Hadamard Transform}

The computation above shows a general trick. If the second (target) qubit of $U_f$ is in the state
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)$, then for every input $x$
\[U_f \, \ket{x} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right)
    = (-1)^{f(x)} \, \ket{x} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0} - \ket{1}\right),\]
i.e., the target qubit is unchanged and the value $f(x)$ shows up as a sign in front of $\ket{x}$.
Since the target qubit never changes, we drop it and write the query in the short-hand
\emph{phase flip} form
\[U_f \colon \ket{x} \longmapsto (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x},\]
instead of the original form $U_f \colon \ket{a}\ket{b} \mapsto \ket{a}\ket{b \oplus f(a)}$. By
linearity, $U_f$ maps a superposition $\sum_x \alpha_x \ket{x}$ to $\sum_x \alpha_x (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x}$:
one query flips the sign of every $\ket{x}$ with $f(x) = 1$ at once.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-Qubit Hadamard Transform}

To generalize Deutsch's algorithm to $n$ bits we need the Hadamard gate applied to each of $n$ qubits.
Start with $n = 2$ and let $\ket{x} = \ket{x_1 x_2} = \ket{x_1}\ket{x_2}$. Using
\cref{eq:mixed-0915} and then \cref{eq:hadamard-0924} on each qubit,
\begin{align*}
    (H \otimes H)\ket{x}
     & = H\ket{x_1} \otimes H\ket{x_2}                                                                 \\
     & = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_{y_1 \in \set{0,1}} (-1)^{x_1 y_1} \ket{y_1}\right)
    \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \sum_{y_2 \in \set{0,1}} (-1)^{x_2 y_2} \ket{y_2}\right)                 \\
     & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{y \in \set{0,1}^2} (-1)^{x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2} \ket{y}
    = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{y \in \set{0,1}^2} (-1)^{x \cdot y} \ket{y},
\end{align*}
where $x \cdot y = x_1 y_1 \oplus x_2 y_2$ is the inner product of the bit strings modulo $2$ (only the
parity of the exponent matters). In general, for $x, y \in \set{0,1}^n$ we write
\[x \cdot y = x_1 y_1 \oplus x_2 y_2 \oplus \dots \oplus x_n y_n.\]

\begin{lemma}[Walsh--Hadamard Transform]\label{lem:wh-0924}
    The $n$-qubit Hadamard transform $H^{\otimes n} = H \otimes H \otimes \dots \otimes H$ (the
    \emph{Walsh--Hadamard transform}) acts on a basis state $\ket{x} = \ket{x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n}$ as
    \[H^{\otimes n} \ket{x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{y \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{x \cdot y} \ket{y}.\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Exactly as for $n = 2$: apply \cref{eq:hadamard-0924} to each of the $n$ qubits and multiply out
    the $n$ sums. The exponent is $x_1 y_1 + \dots + x_n y_n$, and $(-1)$ to this power only depends on
    its parity $x \cdot y$.
\end{proof}

The most important special case is $x = 0^n = 00 \cdots 0$: then $x \cdot y = 0$ for every $y$, and
\[H^{\otimes n} \ket{00 \cdots 0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} \ket{x}\]
is the uniform superposition of all $2^n$ bit strings.

\section{Deutsch--Jozsa Algorithm}

\begin{problem}[Deutsch--Jozsa Problem]
    Given $f \colon \set{0,1}^n \to \set{0,1}$ as a black box, with the \emph{promise} that $f$ is
    either constant or balanced (i.e., $f(x) = 0$ for exactly half of the $2^n$ inputs), determine
    which is the case.
\end{problem}

This is the same problem as Deutsch's, but on $n$ bits. It is a \emph{promise problem}: we are told in
advance that $f$ is one of the two kinds. Let $N = 2^n$ be the number of inputs.

\subsection{Classical Query Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Deterministic:} in the worst case $2^{n-1} + 1 = N/2 + 1$ queries are needed. If an
          algorithm has seen only $N/2$ values and they are all equal, then $f$ may still be either
          constant or balanced. The same holds for a randomized algorithm that has to succeed with
          probability $1$: in the worst case it needs $2^{n-1} + 1$ queries.
    \item \emph{Randomized (Monte Carlo):} query $f$ on $\alpha$ independent uniformly random inputs. If
          two of the answers differ, output ``balanced''; otherwise output ``constant''. If $f$ is
          constant, we are always right. If $f$ is balanced, every answer is $0$ or $1$ with probability
          $\sfrac{1}{2}$ each, so all $\alpha$ answers agree, and we fail, with probability
          \[2 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\alpha} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\alpha - 1}.\]
          A constant number of queries gives a constant error probability, and $\alpha = \bigO{\log N}$
          queries bring the error probability down to $\sfrac{1}{N}$.
    \item \emph{Quantum:} the Deutsch--Jozsa algorithm needs $1$ query (and $n$ measurements) and makes
          no error.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Algorithm}

The circuit (\cref{fig:dj-0924}) is Deutsch's circuit with $n$ input qubits: $n$ qubits in $\ket{0}$
and one qubit in $\ket{1}$ go through Hadamard gates, then through $U_f$ (one query), then the $n$
input qubits go through Hadamard gates again and are measured. If any of the $n$ measurements gives
$1$, then $f$ is balanced; if they all give $0$, then $f$ is constant.

\begin{theorem}[Deutsch--Jozsa Algorithm]\label{thm:dj-0924}
    In the circuit of \cref{fig:dj-0924}, measuring the $n$ input qubits gives $y = 0^n$ with
    probability $1$ if $f$ is constant, and with probability $0$ if $f$ is balanced. Hence, if any
    measurement gives $1$, then $f$ is balanced; otherwise $f$ is constant.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We use the phase flip form of $U_f$: the last qubit is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0} - \ket{1})$
    throughout and is dropped. The $n$ input qubits start in $\ket{00 \cdots 0}$. After the first
    layer of Hadamard gates,
    \[H^{\otimes n} \ket{00 \cdots 0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} \ket{x}.\]
    The query $U_f$ flips the sign of every $\ket{x}$ with $f(x) = 1$:
    \[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x}.\]
    After the second layer of Hadamard gates, by \cref{lem:wh-0924},
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{f(x)}
        \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}} \sum_{y \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{x \cdot y} \ket{y}
         & = \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{y \in \set{0,1}^n} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{f(x) + x \cdot y} \ket{y}.
    \end{align*}
    Look at the amplitude of $\ket{y}$ for $y = 0^n$. Since $x \cdot 0^n = 0$ for every $x$, it is
    \[\frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{x \in \set{0,1}^n} (-1)^{f(x)}
        = \begin{cases}
            \pm 1 & \text{if $f$ is constant (all $2^n$ terms are equal)},         \\
            0     & \text{if $f$ is balanced (half the terms are $+1$, half are $-1$)}.
        \end{cases}\]
    If $f$ is constant, the state is $\pm \ket{00 \cdots 0}$ (all other amplitudes must be $0$, since
    the amplitudes squared sum to $1$), so the measurement gives $0^n$ with probability $1$. If $f$ is
    balanced, the amplitude of $\ket{00 \cdots 0}$ is $0$, so the measurement gives $0^n$ with
    probability $0$, i.e., at least one of the $n$ measurements gives $1$.
\end{proof}

For $n = 1$ this is exactly Deutsch's algorithm. In both cases a single query suffices, whereas any
classical algorithm with zero error needs $2^{n-1} + 1$ queries.

In this lecture, we finish the analysis of \emph{Grover's search algorithm} from the lecture of
October~6. We look at the two operators of a Grover iteration geometrically: the phase flip $Z_f$ and
the diffusion operator $D$ are two \emph{reflections} of a two-dimensional plane, and two reflections
make a rotation. This gives the state after $k$ iterations and the right number of iterations,
$k = \floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}}$. We then show that a \emph{random} number of iterations
succeeds with constant probability even when the number $a$ of solutions is not known, and we
generalize Grover's algorithm to \emph{quantum amplitude amplification}.

\section{Recap: Grover's Algorithm}

We keep the notation of the last lecture: $f \colon \set{0,1}^n \to \set{0,1}$ has $a$ solutions
and $b = N - a$ non-solutions, where $N = 2^n$ and $1 \leq a \leq N - 1$; $\ket{A}$ and $\ket{B}$ are the
uniform superpositions of the solutions and of the non-solutions; $Z_f \ket{x} = (-1)^{f(x)} \ket{x}$ is
the phase flip (one query) and $Z_0 = I - 2\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}$, where $\ket{0^n}$ is the column vector
$(1, 0, \dots, 0)^T$. Grover's algorithm (\cref{fig:grover-circuit-1006}) prepares
$\ket{h} = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}$, applies the \emph{Grover iteration}
\[G = \underbrace{-H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n}}_{\text{diffusion } D} \;
    \underbrace{Z_f}_{\text{phase flip}}\]
$k$ times, where $k$ is about $\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}$, and measures. The number of iterations depends on
$a$, so in this form we need to know how many solutions there are; \cref{sec:randomk-1008} removes
this assumption. By \cref{eq:h-AB-1006},
\[\ket{h} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{N}} \ket{A} + \sqrt{\frac{b}{N}} \ket{B} = \sin\theta \ket{A} + \cos\theta \ket{B},
    \qquad \theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{a}{N}}\right) \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right).\]
For a unique solution $\theta \approx \sfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$; precisely,
$\sfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \leq \theta < \sfrac{1.12}{\sqrt{N}}$ for $N \geq 2$, by $\sin\theta \leq \theta$ and by
\cref{eq:theta2-1008} below. The whole computation stays in the plane spanned by $\ket{A}$ and
$\ket{B}$: we write a unit vector of this plane with real coefficients as
$\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}$ and call $\alpha$ its \emph{angle} (measured from $\ket{B}$
towards $\ket{A}$). The initial state $\ket{h}$ has angle $\theta$, and a measurement of the state with
angle $\alpha$ gives a solution with probability $\sin^2\alpha$.

\section{A Grover Iteration Is Two Reflections}

\subsection{The Phase Flip Is the Reflection About \texorpdfstring{$\ket{B}$}{|B>}}

The phase flip negates every solution and fixes every non-solution, so
\[Z_f \ket{A} = -\ket{A}, \qquad Z_f \ket{B} = \ket{B}.\]
The operator $I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A}$ does the same to these two vectors, because
$\braket{A}{A} = 1$ and $\braket{A}{B} = 0$:
$(I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A})\ket{A} = \ket{A} - 2\ket{A} = -\ket{A}$ and
$(I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A})\ket{B} = \ket{B}$. Two linear operators that agree on $\ket{A}$ and $\ket{B}$
agree on the whole plane, so on the plane
\[Z_f = I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A}.\]
(On all of the $N$-dimensional space $Z_f = I - 2\sum_{x \suchthat f(x) = 1} \ket{x}\bra{x}$; this is
equal to $I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A}$ everywhere only for a unique solution, but only the plane matters to
us.)

\begin{lemma}[$Z_f$ Reflects About $\ket{B}$]\label{lem:zf-1008}
    For every angle $\alpha$,
    \[Z_f \left(\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}\right)
        = -\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}
        = \sin(-\alpha) \ket{A} + \cos(-\alpha) \ket{B}.\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\ket{\phi} = \sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}$. Then
    $\braket{A}{\phi} = \sin\alpha \braket{A}{A} + \cos\alpha \braket{A}{B} = \sin\alpha$, so
    \begin{align*}
        Z_f \ket{\phi} & = \left(I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A}\right)\left(\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}\right)
        = \sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B} - 2\sin\alpha \ket{A} \\
                       & = -\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}
        = \sin(-\alpha) \ket{A} + \cos(-\alpha) \ket{B},
    \end{align*}
    because the sine is an odd function and the cosine is an even function.
\end{proof}

So $Z_f$ sends the angle $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$: it keeps the $\ket{B}$ component and negates the
$\ket{A}$ component, which is the reflection of the plane about the line of $\ket{B}$. On the board
this computation was later rewritten with $-I + 2\ket{A}\bra{A}$ in place of $I - 2\ket{A}\bra{A}$,
still under the name $Z_f\ket{\phi}$; that is the computation for $-Z_f$, and the lemma above is the
one for $Z_f$ itself. The operator with the opposite sign, which on the plane is
\[-Z_f = 2\ket{A}\bra{A} - I, \qquad
    -Z_f \left(\sin\alpha \ket{A} + \cos\alpha \ket{B}\right) = \sin\alpha \ket{A} - \cos\alpha \ket{B},\]
keeps the $\ket{A}$ component and negates the $\ket{B}$ component: it is the reflection about the line
of $\ket{A}$.

\subsection{The Diffusion Operator Is the Reflection About \texorpdfstring{$\ket{h}$}{|h>}}

Let
\[\ket{h^\perp} = \cos\theta \ket{A} - \sin\theta \ket{B}\]
be the unit vector of the plane that is orthogonal to $\ket{h}$:
$\braket{h}{h^\perp} = \sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta = 0$ and
$\braket{h^\perp}{h^\perp} = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. So $\ket{h}$ and $\ket{h^\perp}$ are a
second orthonormal basis of the plane, turned by the angle $\theta$ against the basis $\ket{B}$,
$\ket{A}$ (\cref{fig:reflections-1008}).

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:diffusion-1008}
    Show that $D = 2\ket{h}\bra{h} - I$, and that for every state
    $\ket{\phi} = c \ket{h} + d \ket{h^\perp}$ of the plane,
    \[D \ket{\phi} = c \ket{h} - d \ket{h^\perp}.\]
    (In class the two coefficients were called $a$ and $b$; we write $c$ and $d$ because $a$ and $b$
    are already the numbers of solutions and non-solutions.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{lemma}[$D$ Reflects About $\ket{h}$]\label{lem:d-1008}
    $H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} = I - 2\ket{h}\bra{h}$, hence
    $D = -H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} = 2\ket{h}\bra{h} - I$, and for all numbers $c$ and $d$
    \[D \left(c \ket{h} + d \ket{h^\perp}\right) = c \ket{h} - d \ket{h^\perp},
        \qquad
        H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} \left(c \ket{h} + d \ket{h^\perp}\right) = -c \ket{h} + d \ket{h^\perp}.\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    $H^{\otimes n}$ is its own inverse and its own conjugate transpose, so
    $H^{\otimes n} I H^{\otimes n} = I$, and $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \ket{h}$ gives
    $\bra{0^n} H^{\otimes n} = \bra{h}$. Therefore, as in \cref{lem:diffusion-1006},
    \[H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} = H^{\otimes n}\left(I - 2\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}\right)H^{\otimes n}
        = I - 2 H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}H^{\otimes n} = I - 2\ket{h}\bra{h},\]
    and $D$ is the negative of this operator. Now let $\ket{\phi} = c\ket{h} + d\ket{h^\perp}$. Since
    $\braket{h}{h} = 1$ and $\braket{h}{h^\perp} = 0$, we have $\braket{h}{\phi} = c$, so
    \[D\ket{\phi} = 2\ket{h}\braket{h}{\phi} - \ket{\phi} = 2c\ket{h} - c\ket{h} - d\ket{h^\perp}
        = c\ket{h} - d\ket{h^\perp},\]
    and $H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n}\ket{\phi} = -D\ket{\phi} = -c\ket{h} + d\ket{h^\perp}$.
\end{proof}

So $D$ keeps the $\ket{h}$ component and negates the $\ket{h^\perp}$ component: it is the reflection of
the plane about the line of $\ket{h}$. Without the minus sign, $H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} = I - 2\ket{h}\bra{h}$
negates the $\ket{h}$ component instead, so it is the reflection about the line of $\ket{h^\perp}$; in
the form written in the notes,
\[\left(I - 2\ket{h}\bra{h}\right)\left(\sin\alpha \ket{h} + \cos\alpha \ket{h^\perp}\right)
    = \sin(-\alpha) \ket{h} + \cos\alpha \ket{h^\perp}.\]

\paragraph{The sign of $G$.} In class $D$ was first written as $H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} = I - 2\ket{h}\bra{h}$
and then corrected to $D = 2\ket{h}\bra{h} - I$, and the Grover iteration was written as
$G = H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n} \left(-Z_f\right)$. This is the same operator as the
$G = D Z_f$ of the last lecture, because the minus sign can be moved from one factor to the other:
\[G = \left(-H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n}\right) Z_f = \left(H^{\otimes n} Z_0 H^{\otimes n}\right)\left(-Z_f\right).\]
In the first form $G$ is the reflection about $\ket{B}$ followed by the reflection about $\ket{h}$; in
the second form it is the reflection about $\ket{A}$ followed by the reflection about $\ket{h^\perp}$.
Both pairs of lines make the angle $\theta$, and both describe the same $G$. (On the board the
label ``reflection $\ket{B}$'' stayed under the phase flip after the minus sign was moved to it;
that label belongs to $Z_f$, and the factor $-Z_f$ reflects about $\ket{A}$.)

\subsection{Two Reflections Make a Rotation}


\Cref{fig:reflections-1008} shows what one iteration does to $\ket{h}$: the first reflection takes the
angle $\theta$ to $-\theta$, and the second reflection, about the line at angle $\theta$, takes the
angle $-\theta$, which is $2\theta$ below that line, to the angle $2\theta$ above it, i.e., to
$3\theta$. The same happens to every state of the plane. We prove this once in a general form, for
any two orthonormal vectors, so that we can use it again in \cref{sec:ampamp-1008}.

\begin{lemma}[Two Reflections Make a Rotation]\label{lem:tworef-1008}
    Let $\ket{A}$ and $\ket{B}$ be orthonormal vectors, let $\theta$ be a real number and
    $\ket{\psi} = \sin\theta \ket{A} + \cos\theta \ket{B}$. Let $S$ be a linear operator with
    $S\ket{A} = -\ket{A}$ and $S\ket{B} = \ket{B}$, and let $T = 2\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi} - I$. Then for
    every real number $\beta$,
    \[T S \left(\sin\beta \ket{A} + \cos\beta \ket{B}\right)
        = \sin(\beta + 2\theta) \ket{A} + \cos(\beta + 2\theta) \ket{B},\]
    and therefore, for every integer $k \geq 0$,
    \[(TS)^k \ket{\psi} = \sin\left((2k+1)\theta\right) \ket{A} + \cos\left((2k+1)\theta\right) \ket{B}.\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    \emph{The reflection $S$.} By linearity,
    $S(\sin\beta \ket{A} + \cos\beta \ket{B}) = -\sin\beta \ket{A} + \cos\beta \ket{B}
        = \sin\gamma \ket{A} + \cos\gamma \ket{B}$ with $\gamma = -\beta$.

    \emph{The reflection $T$.} Let $\ket{\chi} = \sin\gamma \ket{A} + \cos\gamma \ket{B}$. By
    orthonormality,
    \[\braket{\psi}{\chi} = \sin\theta\sin\gamma + \cos\theta\cos\gamma = \cos(\gamma - \theta),\]
    so
    \begin{align*}
        T\ket{\chi} & = 2\cos(\gamma - \theta)\ket{\psi} - \ket{\chi}                                     \\
                    & = \left(2\cos(\gamma - \theta)\sin\theta - \sin\gamma\right)\ket{A}
        + \left(2\cos(\gamma - \theta)\cos\theta - \cos\gamma\right)\ket{B}.
    \end{align*}
    By the product formulas $2\cos u \sin v = \sin(u + v) - \sin(u - v)$ and
    $2\cos u \cos v = \cos(u + v) + \cos(u - v)$ with $u = \gamma - \theta$ and $v = \theta$,
    \begin{align*}
        2\cos(\gamma - \theta)\sin\theta - \sin\gamma & = \sin\gamma - \sin(\gamma - 2\theta) - \sin\gamma = \sin(2\theta - \gamma), \\
        2\cos(\gamma - \theta)\cos\theta - \cos\gamma & = \cos\gamma + \cos(\gamma - 2\theta) - \cos\gamma = \cos(2\theta - \gamma).
    \end{align*}
    So $T$ sends the angle $\gamma$ to $2\theta - \gamma$, the mirror image of $\gamma$ about $\theta$.

    \emph{Together.} With $\gamma = -\beta$ we get the angle $2\theta - (-\beta) = \beta + 2\theta$, which
    is the first claim. For the second claim, $\ket{\psi}$ has the angle $\theta$, and each application
    of $TS$ adds $2\theta$, so by induction on $k$ the state $(TS)^k\ket{\psi}$ has the angle
    $\theta + 2k\theta = (2k+1)\theta$.
\end{proof}

For Grover's algorithm we take $S = Z_f$ (which satisfies $Z_f\ket{A} = -\ket{A}$ and
$Z_f\ket{B} = \ket{B}$), $\ket{\psi} = \ket{h}$ and $T = D = 2\ket{h}\bra{h} - I$
(\cref{lem:d-1008}), so that $TS = D Z_f = G$:

\begin{corollary}[The State After $k$ Iterations]\label{cor:hk-1008}
    Every Grover iteration rotates the plane by $2\theta$ from $\ket{B}$ towards $\ket{A}$, and the
    state after $k$ iterations is
    \[\ket{h^k} = G^k\ket{h} = \sin\left((2k+1)\theta\right) \ket{A} + \cos\left((2k+1)\theta\right) \ket{B}.\]
    A measurement of $\ket{h^k}$ gives a solution with probability $\sin^2\left((2k+1)\theta\right)$.
\end{corollary}

This is the formula of \cref{ex:grover-rotation-1006}, now from the geometry alone.

\subsection{The Matrix of a Grover Iteration}

In \cref{lem:giter-1006} we computed
\[G\ket{A} = \left(1 - \frac{2a}{N}\right)\ket{A} - \frac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N}\ket{B},
    \qquad
    G\ket{B} = \frac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N}\ket{A} - \left(1 - \frac{2b}{N}\right)\ket{B}.\]
Since $a + b = N$, both diagonal coefficients are equal to $\sfrac{(b-a)}{N}$:
\[1 - \frac{2a}{N} = \frac{N - 2a}{N} = \frac{b - a}{N},
    \qquad
    -\left(1 - \frac{2b}{N}\right) = \frac{2b - N}{N} = \frac{b - a}{N}.\]
So in the basis $(\ket{B}, \ket{A})$, where the state $\beta\ket{B} + \alpha\ket{A}$ is the column
vector $(\beta, \alpha)^T$, the Grover iteration is the matrix
\[M = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{b - a}{N} & -\dfrac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} \\[2ex] \dfrac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} & \dfrac{b - a}{N} \end{pmatrix}
    = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\theta & -\sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & \cos 2\theta \end{pmatrix}.\]

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:msquare-1008}
    Show that
    \[M = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{\sfrac{b}{N}} & -\sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}} \\[1ex] \sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}} & \sqrt{\sfrac{b}{N}} \end{pmatrix}^{2}
        = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}^{2},\]
    i.e., that the Grover iteration is the rotation by $\theta$ applied twice.
\end{exercise}

\begin{wsolution}
    Let $R_\theta = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{smallmatrix}\right)$
    with $\cos\theta = \sqrt{\sfrac{b}{N}}$ and $\sin\theta = \sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}}$. Multiplying,
    \begin{align*}
        R_\theta^2
         & = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta & -2\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ 2\sin\theta\cos\theta & \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}
        = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{b}{N} - \dfrac{a}{N} & -2\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{N}}\sqrt{\dfrac{b}{N}} \\[2ex] 2\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{N}}\sqrt{\dfrac{b}{N}} & \dfrac{b}{N} - \dfrac{a}{N} \end{pmatrix} \\[1ex]
         & = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{b - a}{N} & -\dfrac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} \\[2ex] \dfrac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} & \dfrac{b - a}{N} \end{pmatrix} = M.
    \end{align*}
    By the double angle formulas the entries are $\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$ and
    $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so $M = R_\theta^2 = R_{2\theta}$.

    Hence $M^k = R_{2k\theta}$, which is \cref{cor:hk-1008} in matrix form: $\ket{h}$ is the first
    column $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^T$ of $R_\theta$, so $M^k\ket{h}$ is the first column of $R_{(2k+1)\theta}$.
\end{wsolution}

\section{The Number of Iterations}\label{sec:iterations-1008}

By \cref{cor:hk-1008} the success probability after $k$ iterations is $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, so we
want the angle $(2k+1)\theta$ to be $\sfrac{\pi}{2}$:
\[(2k+1)\theta \approx \frac{\pi}{2}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2k + 1 \approx \frac{\pi}{2\theta}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k \approx \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac{1}{2}.\]
(Dividing $2k + 1 \approx \sfrac{\pi}{2\theta}$ by $2$ gives $\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}$; the handwritten
copy of the last line has $\sfrac{\pi}{2\theta}$, which is a copying mistake.) So $k$ is
$\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}$ up to rounding, which is the $k = \sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}$ of the recap
with the right constant. For a unique solution ($a = 1$), $\theta \approx \sfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$, so
$k \approx \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N} - \frac{1}{2}$, and we take
\[k = \floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}}; \qquad \text{for all } a\colon \quad k = \floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{a}}}.\]

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:iterations-1008}
    The best number of iterations is $k \approx \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac{1}{2}$. But $k$ has to be
    an integer, and the formulas above replace $\theta$ by
    $\sin\theta = \sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}}$. Show that Grover's algorithm with
    $k = \floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}}$ iterations still finds a solution with probability
    close to $1$.
\end{exercise}

In the last lecture (\cref{thm:grover-1006}) we proved this for $k = \floor{\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}}$,
which needs the angle $\theta$ itself. The number written in class is slightly different, so it needs
its own proof. On the board the brackets around $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}$ can also be read as
rounding up or as rounding to the nearest integer, so the theorem covers these readings too.

\begin{theorem}[Number of Iterations]\label{thm:iterations-1008}
    Let $f$ have $a$ solutions, $1 \leq a \leq \sfrac{N}{2}$, and let
    $\kappa = \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Grover's algorithm with $k = \floor{\kappa}$ iterations makes $k \geq 1$ queries and
              outputs a solution with probability at least $1 - \sfrac{2a}{N}$. For a unique solution
              this is $k = \floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}}$ and probability at least $1 - \sfrac{2}{N}$.
        \item For every integer $k \geq 0$ with $\abs{k - \kappa} < 1$, in particular for
              $k = \ceil{\kappa}$ and for $\kappa$ rounded to the nearest integer, the algorithm
              outputs a solution with probability at least $1 - \sfrac{14a}{N}$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $t = \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac{1}{2}$ be the real number with $(2t+1)\theta = \sfrac{\pi}{2}$
    (the value of $k$ derived above). For every integer $k$ we have
    $(2k+1)\theta = (2t+1)\theta + 2(k-t)\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2(k-t)\theta$, so by \cref{cor:hk-1008},
    $\sin(\sfrac{\pi}{2} + x) = \cos x$ and $\abs{\sin x} \leq \abs{x}$, the success probability is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:succ-1008}
        \sin^2\left((2k+1)\theta\right) = \cos^2\left(2(k-t)\theta\right) = 1 - \sin^2\left(2(k-t)\theta\right)
        \geq 1 - 4(k-t)^2\theta^2.
    \end{equation}
    So we have to bound the distance $\abs{k - t}$ and the angle $\theta$.

    \emph{Two facts about $\theta$.} Since $\sin\theta = \sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}} \leq \sfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$,
    we have $0 < \theta \leq \sfrac{\pi}{4}$, so $\theta < 0.79$ and $\theta^2 < 0.62$. We use the
    inequalities
    \[\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{6} \leq \sin\theta \leq \theta \qquad \text{for } \theta \geq 0.\]
    (Proof: $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(\sfrac{x}{2}) \leq \sfrac{x^2}{2}$, so the function
    $g(x) = \sin x - x + \sfrac{x^3}{6}$ has $g(0) = 0$ and $g'(x) = \cos x - 1 + \sfrac{x^2}{2} \geq 0$,
    hence $g(x) \geq 0$ for $x \geq 0$; the upper bound $\sin x \leq x$ was used in the last lecture.)
    The lower bound gives $\sin\theta \geq \theta\left(1 - \sfrac{\theta^2}{6}\right)$, where
    $1 - \sfrac{\theta^2}{6} > 1 - \sfrac{0.62}{6} > 0.896$. Hence, first,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:theta2-1008}
        \theta^2 \leq \frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \sfrac{\theta^2}{6})^2} < \frac{\sin^2\theta}{0.896^2} < \frac{5}{4}\sin^2\theta = \frac{5a}{4N},
    \end{equation}
    because $0.896^2 > 0.8$. Second, $\kappa = \frac{\pi}{4\sin\theta}$, and
    \[0 \leq \frac{1}{\sin\theta} - \frac{1}{\theta}
        \leq \frac{1}{\theta\left(1 - \sfrac{\theta^2}{6}\right)} - \frac{1}{\theta}
        = \frac{6 - (6 - \theta^2)}{\theta\left(6 - \theta^2\right)}
        = \frac{\theta}{6 - \theta^2} < \frac{0.79}{5.38} < 0.15,\]
    so, multiplying by $\sfrac{\pi}{4} < 0.79$,
    \[0 \leq \kappa - \frac{\pi}{4\theta} < 0.79 \cdot 0.15 < \frac{1}{8},
        \qquad \text{and therefore} \qquad
        \frac{1}{2} \leq \kappa - t < \frac{5}{8}.\]

    \emph{Part 1.} For $k = \floor{\kappa}$ we have $-1 < k - \kappa \leq 0$, so
    $k - t = (k - \kappa) + (\kappa - t)$ satisfies $-\frac{1}{2} < k - t < \frac{5}{8}$, i.e.,
    $(k - t)^2 < \sfrac{25}{64}$. By \cref{eq:succ-1008,eq:theta2-1008} the success probability is at
    least
    \[1 - 4 \cdot \frac{25}{64} \cdot \frac{5a}{4N} = 1 - \frac{125}{64} \cdot \frac{a}{N} \geq 1 - \frac{2a}{N}.\]
    Moreover $\kappa \geq \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{2} > 1$ because $\sfrac{N}{a} \geq 2$, so $k \geq 1$.

    \emph{Part 2.} If $\abs{k - \kappa} < 1$, then $-\frac{1}{2} < k - t < 1 + \frac{5}{8} = \frac{13}{8}$,
    i.e., $(k-t)^2 < \sfrac{169}{64}$, and the success probability is at least
    \[1 - 4 \cdot \frac{169}{64} \cdot \frac{5a}{4N} = 1 - \frac{845}{64} \cdot \frac{a}{N} \geq 1 - \frac{14a}{N}.\]
    Both $\ceil{\kappa}$ and the nearest integer to $\kappa$ are at distance less than $1$ from
    $\kappa$.
\end{proof}

The number $\kappa$ is slightly larger than the
$\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}$ of \cref{thm:grover-1006} (by less than $\sfrac{1}{8}$, as the proof shows), so
the two choices of $k$ are equal or differ by one.

Rounding down is the right reading. Rounding up is as good only while $\sfrac{a}{N}$ is small: the
bound $1 - \sfrac{14a}{N}$ of part~2 says nothing for $a \geq \sfrac{N}{14}$, and indeed for
$a = \sfrac{N}{4}$ we have $\theta = \sfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\kappa = \sfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57$, so that
$\floor{\kappa} = 1$ iteration gives the angle $3\theta = \sfrac{\pi}{2}$ and success probability $1$
(this is the two-qubit example of the last lecture), while $\ceil{\kappa} = 2$ iterations overshoot to
the angle $5\theta = \sfrac{5\pi}{6}$ and success probability $\sin^2(\sfrac{5\pi}{6}) = \sfrac{1}{4}$.

\section{A Random Number of Iterations}\label{sec:randomk-1008}

The number of iterations $\floor{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}}$ depends on the number $a$ of
solutions, which we may not know. A wrong $k$ can be useless, because the state keeps rotating. The
way out is to choose $k$ \emph{at random}.

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:randomk-1008}
    Prove that if $k$ is chosen uniformly at random from $\set{1, \dots, 2\sqrt{N}}$ and then $k$
    Grover iterations are made, then $\prob{\text{success}} = \bigTh{1}$.
\end{exercise}

On the board the set was written as $\set{1, \dots, \sqrt{N}}$ and in the notes as
$\set{1, \dots, 2\sqrt{N}}$; we prove the statement for both. Since $\sqrt{N}$ need not be an
integer, the sets are $\set{1, \dots, \floor{\sqrt{N}}}$ and $\set{1, \dots, \floor{2\sqrt{N}}}$. The
algorithm does not use $a$: for the larger set the theorem holds for every number of solutions, and
for the smaller set for every number of solutions up to $\sfrac{N}{2}$.

\begin{theorem}[Grover's Algorithm with a Random Number of Iterations]\label{thm:randomk-1008}
    Let $N = 2^n$ and let $f$ have $a$ solutions, $1 \leq a \leq \sfrac{N}{2}$. Let $K \geq 1$ be an
    integer, choose $k$ uniformly at random from $\set{1, \dots, K}$, make $k$ Grover iterations on
    $\ket{h}$ and measure. Then the probability of measuring a solution is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:avg-1008}
        \prob{\text{success}} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\sin\left(4(K+1)\theta\right) - \sin 4\theta}{4K\sin 2\theta}
        \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4K\sin 2\theta}.
    \end{equation}
    In particular,
    \[\prob{\text{success}} \geq \frac{1}{4} \quad \text{for } K = \floor{\sqrt{N}},
        \qquad
        \prob{\text{success}} \geq \frac{3}{8} \quad \text{for } K = \floor{2\sqrt{N}}.\]
    In both cases $\sfrac{1}{4} \leq \prob{\text{success}} \leq 1$, i.e., $\prob{\text{success}} = \bigTh{1}$,
    and the algorithm makes at most $2\sqrt{N} = \bigO{\sqrt{N}}$ queries. Moreover, for
    $K = \floor{2\sqrt{N}}$ the bound $\prob{\text{success}} \geq \sfrac{1}{4}$ holds for every number of
    solutions $1 \leq a \leq N - 1$, without the assumption $a \leq \sfrac{N}{2}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    \emph{The average.} Given the value of $k$, the success probability is $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$ by
    \cref{cor:hk-1008}, and each value has probability $\sfrac{1}{K}$. By the law of total probability
    and $\sin^2 x = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \cos 2x)$,
    \[\prob{\text{success}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \sin^2\left((2k+1)\theta\right)
        = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \cos\left((4k+2)\theta\right).\]

    \emph{The sum of cosines.} By the product formula $2\cos u \sin v = \sin(u+v) - \sin(u-v)$ with
    $u = (4k+2)\theta$ and $v = 2\theta$,
    \[2\sin 2\theta \cos\left((4k+2)\theta\right) = \sin\left(4(k+1)\theta\right) - \sin\left(4k\theta\right).\]
    Summing over $k = 1, \dots, K$, the right-hand side telescopes:
    \[2\sin 2\theta \sum_{k=1}^{K} \cos\left((4k+2)\theta\right) = \sin\left(4(K+1)\theta\right) - \sin 4\theta.\]
    As $0 < \theta \leq \sfrac{\pi}{4}$ (because $\sin\theta = \sqrt{\sfrac{a}{N}} \leq \sfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$),
    we have $0 < 2\theta \leq \sfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $\sin 2\theta > 0$, so we may divide by
    $2\sin 2\theta$, which gives the equality in \cref{eq:avg-1008}.

    \emph{The lower bound.} $\sin(4(K+1)\theta) \leq 1$, and $\sin 4\theta \geq 0$ because
    $0 < 4\theta \leq \pi$. So the numerator $\sin(4(K+1)\theta) - \sin 4\theta$ is at most $1$, which
    gives the inequality in \cref{eq:avg-1008}.

    \emph{The two choices of $K$.} Since $b = N - a \geq \sfrac{N}{2}$,
    \[\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} \geq \frac{2\sqrt{a \cdot \sfrac{N}{2}}}{N} = \sqrt{\frac{2a}{N}}.\]
    Next, $\floor{\sqrt{N}} \geq \sqrt{\sfrac{N}{2}}$ and $\floor{2\sqrt{N}} \geq \sqrt{2N}$ for
    $N = 2^n$: if $n$ is even, then $\sqrt{N} = 2^{n/2}$ and $2\sqrt{N}$ are integers and the floors
    change nothing; if $n$ is odd, then $\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{2}} = 2^{(n-1)/2}$ and $\sqrt{2N} = 2^{(n+1)/2}$
    are integers with $\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{2}} \leq \sqrt{N}$ and $\sqrt{2N} \leq 2\sqrt{N}$, so they are at
    most the floors. Therefore
    \begin{align*}
        K = \floor{\sqrt{N}}\colon  & \quad K\sin 2\theta \geq \sqrt{\frac{N}{2}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2a}{N}} = \sqrt{a} \geq 1,
                                    &                                                                                         & \prob{\text{success}} \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}, \\
        K = \floor{2\sqrt{N}}\colon & \quad K\sin 2\theta \geq \sqrt{2N} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2a}{N}} = 2\sqrt{a} \geq 2,
                                    &                                                                                         & \prob{\text{success}} \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8}.
    \end{align*}
    A probability is at most $1$, so in both cases $\prob{\text{success}} = \bigTh{1}$. The number of
    queries is $k \leq K \leq 2\sqrt{N}$.

    \emph{Every $a$ for $K = \floor{2\sqrt{N}}$.} Now let $1 \leq a \leq N - 1$ be arbitrary, so
    $0 < \theta < \sfrac{\pi}{2}$. Then $\sin 2\theta > 0$ still holds, so the equality in
    \cref{eq:avg-1008} is still true; only the bound on the numerator changes, to
    $\sin(4(K+1)\theta) - \sin 4\theta \leq 2$. The larger one of $a$ and $b$ is at least
    $\sfrac{N}{2}$ and the smaller one is at least $1$, so
    $\sin 2\theta = \sfrac{2\sqrt{ab}}{N} \geq \sfrac{2\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{2}}}{N} = \sqrt{\sfrac{2}{N}}$
    and $K\sin 2\theta \geq \sqrt{2N} \cdot \sqrt{\sfrac{2}{N}} = 2$. Hence
    \[\prob{\text{success}} \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{4K\sin 2\theta} \geq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}. \qedhere\]
\end{proof}

Intuitively, when $k$ runs over a range in which the angle $(2k+1)\theta$ makes at least a constant
fraction of a full turn, the average of $\sin^2$ over the range is close to $\sfrac{1}{2}$, whatever
$\theta$ is. A constant success probability is enough: given the measured $x$ we check $f(x) = 1$ with
one more query and repeat the whole algorithm if the check fails, so the expected number of
repetitions is at most $4$.

For the set $\set{1, \dots, \floor{2\sqrt{N}}}$ of the notes the theorem needs no assumption on $a$. For
the smaller set $\set{1, \dots, \floor{\sqrt{N}}}$ of the board it assumes $a \leq \sfrac{N}{2}$, and an
algorithm that does not know $a$ cannot check this; one more classical query removes the assumption.
If more than half of the inputs are solutions ($a > \sfrac{N}{2}$), a uniformly random $x$ is a
solution with probability more than $\sfrac{1}{2}$. So each round first tests one uniformly random
$x$ with one classical query and, if it is not a solution, runs the algorithm of
\cref{thm:randomk-1008}. A round then succeeds with probability at least $\sfrac{1}{4}$ for every
$1 \leq a \leq N$ (more than $\sfrac{1}{2}$ from the classical sample if $a > \sfrac{N}{2}$, and at
least $\sfrac{1}{4}$ from the theorem otherwise) and makes at most $2\sqrt{N} + 2$ queries, including
the final check.

\paragraph{The number $k$ must be random.} The statement cannot mean that every fixed
$k \in \set{1, \dots, 2\sqrt{N}}$ works: some fixed values of $k$ fail. Let $a = 1$, so that $\sin\theta = \sfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}$. For $k = 1$
the success probability is $\sin^2 3\theta \leq 9\theta^2 < \sfrac{12}{N}$ by \cref{eq:theta2-1008},
which tends to $0$; this $k$ lies in both sets. And if $k$ is the integer nearest to $\frac{\pi}{2\theta} - \frac{1}{2}$, then
$k \leq \frac{\pi}{2\theta} \leq \frac{\pi}{2\sin\theta} = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{N} < 2\sqrt{N}$ and
$\abs{(2k+1)\theta - \pi} \leq \theta$, so the state has rotated half a turn, back to the line of
$\ket{B}$, and the success probability is at most $\sin^2\theta = \sfrac{1}{N}$.

\section{Quantum Amplitude Amplification}\label{sec:ampamp-1008}

Grover's algorithm is a special case of a general method. Let $\mathcal{A}$ be any quantum algorithm
without measurements, i.e., a unitary operator on $n$ qubits, and let $f \colon \set{0,1}^n \to \set{0,1}$
tell us which outcomes $x$ are \emph{good} ($f(x) = 1$) and which are \emph{bad} ($f(x) = 0$). Write
$\mathcal{A}\ket{0^n} = \sum_x c_x \ket{x}$, let
$p = \sum_{x \suchthat f(x) = 1} \abs{c_x}^2$ be the probability that a measurement of
$\mathcal{A}\ket{0^n}$ gives a good outcome, and assume $0 < p < 1$. With the unit vectors
\[\ket{G} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}} \sum_{x \suchthat f(x) = 1} c_x \ket{x},
    \qquad
    \ket{B} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-p}} \sum_{x \suchthat f(x) = 0} c_x \ket{x},\]
which are orthogonal because no basis state appears in both, the state that $\mathcal{A}$ produces is
\[\ket{\phi} = \mathcal{A}\ket{0^n} = \sqrt{p}\, \ket{G} + \sqrt{1-p}\, \ket{B}.\]
\emph{Classically} we would run $\mathcal{A}$, measure, check the outcome with $f$, and repeat until
the outcome is good. Each run succeeds with probability $p$ independently, so the number of runs is
geometric with mean $\sfrac{1}{p}$. \emph{Amplitude amplification} needs only $\bigO{\sfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}}$
runs.

\begin{exercise}\label{ex:ampamp-1008}
    Show that a good outcome can be found with constant probability using $\bigO{\sfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}}$
    applications of $\mathcal{A}$ and of its inverse, instead of the $\sfrac{1}{p}$ repetitions of the
    classical method.
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}[Amplitude Amplification]\label{thm:ampamp-1008}
    Let $\mathcal{A}$, $f$, $p$, $\ket{G}$, $\ket{B}$ and $\ket{\phi}$ be as above, let
    $\theta \in (0, \sfrac{\pi}{2})$ with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{p}$, and let
    \[Q = -\mathcal{A} Z_0 \mathcal{A}^{-1} Z_f.\]
    Then $Q^k\ket{\phi} = \sin\left((2k+1)\theta\right)\ket{G} + \cos\left((2k+1)\theta\right)\ket{B}$
    for every $k \geq 0$. For $k = \floor{\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}}$, a measurement of $Q^k\ket{\phi}$ gives
    a good outcome with probability at least $\max(p, 1 - p) \geq \sfrac{1}{2}$, and computing $Q^k \mathcal{A}\ket{0^n}$ takes
    $2k + 1 \leq \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{p}} + 1 = \bigO{\sfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}}$ applications of $\mathcal{A}$ or
    $\mathcal{A}^{-1}$ and $k$ queries to $f$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    \emph{The two reflections.} $Z_f$ negates every good basis state and fixes every bad one, so
    $Z_f\ket{G} = -\ket{G}$ and $Z_f\ket{B} = \ket{B}$. Since $\mathcal{A}$ is unitary,
    $\mathcal{A}^{-1} = \mathcal{A}^\dagger$, so $\mathcal{A} I \mathcal{A}^{-1} = I$ and
    $\bra{0^n}\mathcal{A}^{-1} = \left(\mathcal{A}\ket{0^n}\right)^\dagger = \bra{\phi}$. Hence
    \[-\mathcal{A} Z_0 \mathcal{A}^{-1} = -\mathcal{A}\left(I - 2\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}\right)\mathcal{A}^{-1}
        = -\left(I - 2\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}\right) = 2\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi} - I.\]
    So $Q = TS$ with $S = Z_f$ and $T = 2\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi} - I$, and
    $\ket{\phi} = \sin\theta\ket{G} + \cos\theta\ket{B}$ because $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta} = \sqrt{1-p}$.
    \Cref{lem:tworef-1008}, applied to the orthonormal vectors $\ket{G}$ and $\ket{B}$ and to
    $\ket{\psi} = \ket{\phi}$, gives the formula for $Q^k\ket{\phi}$.

    \emph{The success probability.} $\ket{G}$ contains only good basis states and $\ket{B}$ only bad
    ones, so a measurement of $Q^k\ket{\phi}$ gives a good outcome with probability
    $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$. For $k = \floor{\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}}$ we have
    $\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta} - 1 < k \leq \sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}$, so
    $\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta < (2k+1)\theta \leq \frac{\pi}{2} + \theta$, and on this interval the sine is
    at least $\sin(\sfrac{\pi}{2} \pm \theta) = \cos\theta$, exactly as in the proof of
    \cref{thm:grover-1006}. So the probability is at least $\cos^2\theta = 1 - p$. It is also at
    least $p$: if $p \leq \sfrac{1}{2}$ then $1 - p \geq p$, and if $p > \sfrac{1}{2}$ then
    $\theta > \sfrac{\pi}{4}$, so $k = 0$ and the probability is $\sin^2\theta = p$.

    \emph{The cost.} One application of $Q$ uses $\mathcal{A}$ once, $\mathcal{A}^{-1}$ once and $Z_f$
    once (one query to $f$); $Z_0$ uses neither. Preparing $\ket{\phi}$ uses $\mathcal{A}$ once more, so
    the total is $2k + 1$ applications, and
    $k \leq \frac{\pi}{4\theta} \leq \frac{\pi}{4\sin\theta} = \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{p}}$ because
    $\sin\theta \leq \theta$.
\end{proof}

The number of iterations $k = \floor{\sfrac{\pi}{4\theta}}$ depends on $p$, so the theorem is for a
known $p$, just as Grover's algorithm with a fixed $k$ needs the number of solutions. (Here $\ket{G}$ is the good state, not the Grover iteration $G$,
and $\ket{B}$ is the bad state of $\mathcal{A}$.) The inverse $\mathcal{A}^{-1}$ is obtained by running the circuit of $\mathcal{A}$ backwards, with
every gate replaced by its inverse; this is why $\mathcal{A}$ must not contain measurements. Grover's
algorithm is the case $\mathcal{A} = H^{\otimes n}$: then $\mathcal{A}^{-1} = H^{\otimes n}$,
$\ket{\phi} = \ket{h}$, $\ket{G} = \ket{A}$, $p = \sfrac{a}{N}$, $Q = G$, and
$\bigO{\sfrac{1}{\sqrt{p}}} = \bigO{\sqrt{\sfrac{N}{a}}}$, compared to the $\sfrac{1}{p} = \sfrac{N}{a}$
expected samples of classical random sampling.
